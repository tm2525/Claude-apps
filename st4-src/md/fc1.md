## 1. Access and technique

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the preferred arterial access for coronary angiography, and its class?</summary><div class="fca">Radial (TSRA: 6 Fr sheath in the right radial).<br>- ESC 2024 CCS: radial access preferred when ICA is indicated, I A<br>- Why: less bleeding, patient comfort; MACE and mortality similar to femoral<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS Rec Table 11; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What drugs are given once arterial access is obtained, before engaging the coronaries?</summary><div class="fca">- Systemic heparin<br>- An arterial vasodilator: nitroglycerin, verapamil or nitroprusside<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Describe how a coronary ostium is engaged for diagnostic angiography.</summary><div class="fca">- Standard 0.035 inch guidewire advanced to the aortic root<br>- Catheter advanced over the wire into the ascending aorta<br>- Wire removed, catheter positioned in the coronary ostium<br>- Contrast injected and images recorded<small>TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which angiographic lesions go on to pressure-wire assessment (TSRA)?</summary><div class="fca">- At least 50% narrowing of the left main<br>- At least 70% narrowing of other major epicardial arteries<br>- Unless ischaemia already documented on outpatient non-invasive testing<br>(ESC 2024: intermediate = 40 to 90% non-LM, 40 to 70% LM)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Outline a PCI after a positive pressure-wire result.</summary><div class="fca">- Further heparin<br>- Exchange pressure wire for a 0.014 inch working wire<br>- Balloon pre-dilatation, deploy drug-eluting stent, post-dilate with a non-compliant balloon<br>- Repeat angiography (IVUS occasionally)<br>- Check ACT before sheath removal; radial pressure device or femoral closure device; P2Y12 bolus in recovery<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How long is dual antiplatelet therapy after PCI?</summary><div class="fca">- ESC 2024 CCS: default after CCS PCI (no OAC indication) aspirin + clopidogrel up to 6 months, I A; high bleeding risk (not high ischaemic risk): stop DAPT at 1 to 3 months, I A<br>- (TSRA ch. 52: 12 months after ACS; at least 6 months in stable disease)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which diagnostic catheters are standard for the left and right coronary arteries?</summary><div class="fca">- Left: Judkins left 4 (JL4); JL4.5 or JL5 if the ascending aorta is dilated<br>- Right: Judkins right 4 (JR4)<br>- JR also used for vein grafts and the LIMA<br>- Amplatz: the other commonly used family<small>Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How do you size the Judkins curve to the aorta and the access route?</summary><div class="fca">- Normal aorta: JL4 (down to 3.5 or up to 4.5 as needed); large aorta JL5<br>- RCA: JR4 (3.5 to 5)<br>- Right radial: JL about 0.5 cm smaller (JL3.5 for JL4); JR about 1 cm larger (JR5 for JR4)<br>- Left radial: conventional curves usually fine<br>- Fit the curve to the root seen on the diagnostic run<small>Pavei and Marco, Tools &amp; Techniques: choice and use of guiding catheters, EuroIntervention 2010;6:543-4 (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>When would you reach for an Amplatz catheter, and what is the catch?</summary><div class="fca">- Large aorta needing more support (AL2 to 3)<br>- Complex circumflex work: deep, coaxial engagement with strong backup; posterior or anterior take-off<br>- Hard-to-cross vein graft to an OM (AL1 to 2)<br>- Superior RCA take-off (AL, AR or hockey stick); AL0.75 for the RCA from the radial<br>- Catch: more aggressive; handle very gently to avoid vessel dissection<small>Pavei and Marco, Tools &amp; Techniques: choice and use of guiding catheters, EuroIntervention 2010;6:543-4 (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which access routes are used for angiography, and how do catheter size and route matter?</summary><div class="fca">- Femoral and radial mostly; brachial rarely (Kesieme); radial preferred, I A (see access card)<br>- 6 Fr allows radial or brachial access and deeper engagement with minimal pressure damping; 7 to 8 Fr damp the pressure<br>- Unusual mammary origin: radial access with IMA or JR catheters<br>- Inferior RCA take-off: JR, short-tip JR, multipurpose or bypass right<small>Pavei and Marco, Tools &amp; Techniques: choice and use of guiding catheters, EuroIntervention 2010;6:543-4 (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full); Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the general rule for cranial versus caudal angulation on the left coronary?</summary><div class="fca">- Cranial views: LAD and the LAD/diagonal bifurcation<br>- Caudal views: circumflex and the proximal vessels<br>- Several views always: bifurcations, foreshortening and overlap cause errors in stenosis estimation<br>(Conflict: Kesieme 2024&#x27;s general section says the opposite, against its own view-by-view list; Naber preferred)<small>Naber et al., Tools &amp; Techniques: angiographic views, EuroIntervention 2010;6(3) (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full); Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which view best shows the circumflex and obtuse marginals?</summary><div class="fca">RAO caudal: origin and whole course of the circumflex with its OM branches (and ramus if present); also the distal left main and proximal LAD.<small>Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full); Naber et al., Tools &amp; Techniques: angiographic views, EuroIntervention 2010;6(3) (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What does the LAO caudal (&#x27;spider&#x27;) view show?</summary><div class="fca">The left main and its bifurcation into the proximal LAD and proximal to mid circumflex (and ramus intermedius if present).<small>Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which views show the LAD, diagonals and septals?</summary><div class="fca">- RAO cranial: left main clearly, mid and distal LAD, diagonals, septal perforators (proximal LAD and circumflex overlap here)<br>- LAO cranial: LAD running parallel to the spine with diagonals and septals; good for diagonal ostia<br>- LAO cranial also shows the PDA well in left dominance<small>Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full); Naber et al., Tools &amp; Techniques: angiographic views, EuroIntervention 2010;6(3) (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which views are used for the right coronary artery?</summary><div class="fca">- LAO first: ostium, proximal and mid RCA; body as a &#x27;C&#x27;<br>- RAO: ostium, proximal and mid RCA<br>- LAO cranial (or AP cranial): distal RCA and the crux, bifurcation into PDA and posterolateral (PLV) branches<small>Naber et al., Tools &amp; Techniques: angiographic views, EuroIntervention 2010;6(3) (DOI not shown on page) (web, read in full); Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

## 2. Coronary anatomy and dominance

<details class="fc"><summary>Describe the course and branches of the left main and LAD.</summary><div class="fca">- Left main: between the pulmonary artery and left atrial appendage; divides into LAD and circumflex<br>- LAD: anterior interventricular groove; septal perforators (anterior two thirds of the septum) and diagonals<small>Cardiac 19 (anatomy)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Describe the course and branches of the circumflex and right coronary artery.</summary><div class="fca">- Circumflex: left AV groove; obtuse marginals; gives the PDA in left dominance<br>- RCA: right AV groove; conal, sinus node and acute marginal branches; at the crux the AV node branch, PDA and posterolateral arteries in right dominance<small>Cardiac 19 (anatomy)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What defines coronary dominance, and how common is each pattern?</summary><div class="fca">The artery that gives the posterior descending artery.<br>- Right dominant: 70 to 85%<br>- Left dominant (circumflex): 10 to 15%<br>(Co-dominance not given in the notes)<small>Cardiac 19 (anatomy)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why does the posteromedial papillary muscle rupture more often than the anterolateral?</summary><div class="fca">- Posteromedial: single supply (RCA or circumflex, via the PDA)<br>- Anterolateral: dual supply (LAD and circumflex)<br>- Posteromedial 75%, anterolateral 25% (TSRA); Atlas draft says 6 to 12 times commoner (conflict, TSRA preferred)<small>Cardiac 19; Cardiac 5 (TSRA ch. 51; FRCS Atlas correction)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which coronary supplies which wall in the TOE mid-papillary short-axis view?</summary><div class="fca">- LAD: anterior wall and anterior septum<br>- Circumflex: lateral walls<br>- RCA: inferior wall and inferior septum (right-dominant heart)<small>Cardiac 9 (TOE and CT)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is co-dominance, and why does dominance matter to the surgeon?</summary><div class="fca">- Co-dominant: the PDA comes off both the RCA and the circumflex (right dominant: RCA gives the PDA; left dominant: circumflex)<br>- Left dominance is associated with higher long-term MACCE after elective surgical revascularisation<br>(Prevalence of co-dominance not given in the source read)<small>Kesieme et al., Basic overview of conventional coronary angiography for planning cardiac surgery, Cureus 2024;16:e52942, doi 10.7759/cureus.52942, PMID 38405998 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

## 3. Grading stenosis severity

<details class="fc"><summary>What angiographic diameter stenosis is conventionally significant?</summary><div class="fca">- 70% or more for all vessels except the left main<br>- 50% or more for the left main<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 50 and 52 (2011 ACCF/AHA)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is an &#x27;intermediate&#x27; stenosis in ESC 2024 CCS?</summary><div class="fca">By visual estimate:<br>- 40 to 90% diameter stenosis in non-left-main vessels<br>- 40 to 70% in the left main<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How well does visual stenosis severity predict FFR (FAME, PRIME-FFR)?</summary><div class="fca">Poorly:<br>- 31% of 40 to 49% stenoses were significant<br>- Only 35% of 50 to 70% were significant<br>- 20% of 71 to 90% were not<br>- Only &gt;90% predicted significance reliably (96% correct)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>When may a lesion be treated without pressure-wire assessment?</summary><div class="fca">- ESC 2024: in very-high-likelihood patients going straight to ICA, stenoses &lt;90% should be judged by pressure assessment, so &gt;=90% may be treated on angiography<br>- TSRA: when ischaemia is already documented on non-invasive testing<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the optimal angiographic cut-offs for a non-significant stenosis, by vessel?</summary><div class="fca">- Left main: 43%<br>- Small vessels: 55%<br>- Large arteries: functional assessment threshold 40% diameter stenosis<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did adding FFR to angiography do in R3F, POST-IT, RIPCORD and DEFINE-REAL?</summary><div class="fca">Reclassified the treatment plan in 30 to 50% of patients, without changing the number recommended for revascularisation.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>On CCTA, what stenosis is obstructive, and what is the pitfall?</summary><div class="fca">- Obstructive usually &gt;=50% or &gt;=70%<br>- Pitfall: many 50 to 69% lesions are not flow-limiting<br>- Heavy calcification in older patients favours functional testing<small>Cardiac 9; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Define in-stent restenosis and its frequency.</summary><div class="fca">- &gt;=50% stenosis within a stent or within 5 mm of its edge<br>- ESC 2024: up to 10% of DES within 10 years; presents as CCS (20% as ACS)<br>- (TSRA: about 5 to 10% over 5 years with 2nd/3rd generation DES)<br>- Treat: ESC prefers DES over drug-coated balloon for in-DES restenosis (I A); TSRA: repeat PCI or CABG<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) and what does it measure?</summary><div class="fca">Computer edge detection of the contrast-filled lumen after calibration (isocentre from DICOM, or a catheter of known size, preferably &gt;=6 Fr; about 0.20 mm/pixel).<br>- Minimal lumen diameter (MLD)<br>- Reference diameter (RD): average diameter of the presumed disease-free vessel<br>- % diameter stenosis = (RD - MLD) / RD<br>- Lesion length<small>Suzuki et al., JACVIT expert consensus on QCA, Cardiovasc Interv Ther 2020;35:105-16, doi 10.1007/s12928-020-00653-7, PMID 32125622 (web, read in part: about 90% of the page)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why use QCA rather than eyeballing?</summary><div class="fca">- Visual grading has large intra- and inter-observer variability: observers agreed on the significance of proximal or mid LAD stenoses in only 65%<br>- Objective, reproducible: needed for core-lab trial analysis<br>- 3D QCA copes better with eccentric lumens and foreshortening and tracks IVUS and FFR more closely<small>Suzuki et al., JACVIT expert consensus on QCA, Cardiovasc Interv Ther 2020;35:105-16, doi 10.1007/s12928-020-00653-7, PMID 32125622 (web, read in part: about 90% of the page)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the limitations of QCA?</summary><div class="fca">- Image quality: poor opacification gives wrong contours; frame choice changes results<br>- Bifurcations: single-vessel algorithms inaccurate (dedicated bifurcation algorithms better)<br>- Diffuse disease: reference diameter and lesion length underestimated<br>- Reads smaller than IVUS and OCT<br>- 2D: foreshortening and overlap (3D QCA partly addresses)<small>Suzuki et al., JACVIT expert consensus on QCA, Cardiovasc Interv Ther 2020;35:105-16, doi 10.1007/s12928-020-00653-7, PMID 32125622 (web, read in part: about 90% of the page)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Define acute gain and late lumen loss.</summary><div class="fca">- Acute gain = post-PCI MLD minus baseline MLD<br>- Late lumen loss = post-PCI MLD minus follow-up MLD<br>- Late loss is an established stent-efficacy endpoint linked to long-term events (used to compare DES generations)<br>- Stent analysis covers the stent plus 5 mm at each edge<small>Suzuki et al., JACVIT expert consensus on QCA, Cardiovasc Interv Ther 2020;35:105-16, doi 10.1007/s12928-020-00653-7, PMID 32125622 (web, read in part: about 90% of the page)</small></div></details>

## 4. Left main and proximal LAD

<details class="fc"><summary>Why are left main and proximal LAD lesions more often FFR-positive, and why does it matter?</summary><div class="fca">- They supply more myocardium<br>- Lower FFR/iFR means proportionally higher risk<br>- For a given FFR, a more proximal lesion carries more risk<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How should an intermediate left main stenosis be assessed before revascularisation (ESC 2024)?</summary><div class="fca">- FFR/iFR should be considered: IIa A<br>- IVUS should be considered: IIa B<br>- IVUS and OCT predict FFR well, especially in the left main; LM plaque burden predicted long-term death in unrevascularised patients<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What angiographic features drive prognosis in chronic coronary disease?</summary><div class="fca">- Number of vessels with &gt;70% stenosis, or a left main stenosis &gt;50% (CASS, ISCHEMIA)<br>- In COURAGE and ISCHEMIA only CAD severity, not ischaemia severity, independently predicted death and MI<br>- LV function is the strongest predictor of long-term survival<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which CCTA findings are high risk and prompt invasive angiography?</summary><div class="fca">- Left main &gt;=50%<br>- Three-vessel &gt;=70%<br>- Two-vessel &gt;=70% including proximal LAD<br>- One-vessel proximal LAD &gt;=70% with FFR-CT &lt;=0.8<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024) Rec Table 14</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>For which anatomy does revascularisation improve survival when LVEF &gt;35% (ESC 2024)?</summary><div class="fca">Revascularisation plus GDMT for functionally significant:<br>- Left main stenosis: I A<br>- Three-vessel disease: I A<br>- One- or two-vessel disease involving the proximal LAD (reduce CV death and MI): I B<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024) Rec Table 22</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What were the 2011 ACCF/AHA Class I anatomical indications for CABG?</summary><div class="fca">- Left main &gt;=50%, or left main equivalent (proximal LAD and one other major coronary artery, as TSRA words it)<br>- Significant stenosis in 3 major coronary arteries<br>(Significant = &gt;=70% diameter)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 50 (2011 ACCF/AHA)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What defines a true left main bifurcation lesion and why does it matter?</summary><div class="fca">- Medina 1,1,1 or 0,1,1: both branches &gt;50% narrowed, reference &gt;=2.75 mm<br>- Often needs two stents; worse outcomes<br>- Distal bifurcation involved in 75% of LM disease<br>- 13% have residual ischaemia despite good angiography after LM bifurcation stenting (higher CV mortality)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

## 5. SYNTAX score

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the SYNTAX score and when does ESC 2024 recommend it?</summary><div class="fca">- Angiographic complexity score for left main and multivessel CAD<br>- Recommended in multivessel obstructive CAD to assess anatomical complexity: I B<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the limitations of the SYNTAX score?</summary><div class="fca">- Time-consuming<br>- Inter-observer variability (poor core-lab versus operator agreement)<br>- Anatomy only: no plaque burden<br>- No physiology or clinical variables<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How does the SYNTAX score set the left main PCI recommendation in ESC 2024?</summary><div class="fca">- &lt;=22 (low): PCI recommended as an alternative to CABG if it can match completeness, I A<br>- 23 to 32 (intermediate): PCI should be considered, IIa A<br>- &gt;=33: 2018 was PCI III B; removed in 2024, replaced by &#x27;high surgical risk: PCI may be considered over medical therapy&#x27; IIb B<br>(Atlas draft calling &gt;=33 Class III in 2024 is wrong)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; FRCS Atlas correction</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did long-term follow-up show for high SYNTAX scores?</summary><div class="fca">- SYNTAXES 10 years: all-cause death similar overall, higher with PCI if SYNTAX &gt;=33 (HR 1.41; 1.05 to 1.89)<br>- LM IPD meta-analysis: trend to higher mortality with PCI if SYNTAX &gt;32 (HR 1.30; 0.92 to 1.84)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What does SYNTAX II add, and how did it perform?</summary><div class="fca">- Adds clinical variables to the anatomical score<br>- Overestimated 4-year mortality in EXCEL<br>- SYNTAX II 2020: built on SYNTAXES; 5-year MACE c-index 0.62 (PCI), 0.67 (CABG); no prospective external validation<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why does residual SYNTAX score matter after PCI?</summary><div class="fca">- SYNTAX PCI arm: higher residual SYNTAX score linked to higher mortality<br>- Anatomically incomplete but functionally complete PCI did better than anatomically complete PCI<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What SYNTAX threshold made CABG Class IIa in the 2011 ACCF/AHA guideline?</summary><div class="fca">Complex three-vessel CAD with SYNTAX score &gt;22, with or without proximal LAD involvement: IIa.<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 50 (2011 ACCF/AHA)</small></div></details>

## 6. Complications

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the major complication rate of diagnostic coronary angiography?</summary><div class="fca">- Femoral: 0.5 to 2.0% major complications (mainly bleeding needing transfusion)<br>- Radial access reduces mortality and major bleeding<br>- Radial composite death, MI or stroke about 0.1 to 0.2%<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which catheter-lab complications need the surgeon, and what is the operation?</summary><div class="fca">- Coronary perforation or dissection from catheter or wire: theatre; graft beyond the injury plus any other significant lesions seen at angiography<br>- STEMI: CABG if PCI cannot be done, disease burden warrants it with immediate theatre, or PCI complication<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Give the frequencies of the main non-surgical PCI complications.</summary><div class="fca">- Major bleeding within 30 days: 2 to 3% (slightly more with femoral)<br>- Periprocedural MI: about 6%<br>- Stroke within 30 days: &lt;1% (not affected by radial v femoral)<br>- In-stent restenosis: 5 to 10% over 5 years<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Periprocedural versus spontaneous MI after PCI: which predicts death?</summary><div class="fca">- Periprocedural (within 24 h; thought due to distal embolisation): NOT a predictor of later mortality<br>- Spontaneous (&gt;=24 h after revascularisation): powerful independent predictor of mortality<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What causes stroke after PCI?</summary><div class="fca">Thromboembolism from calcium deposits in the aortic root (commonly thought). Under 1% at 30 days; access route does not change it.<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How is stent thrombosis managed?</summary><div class="fca">- Usually presents as ACS: urgent ICA, then intracoronary imaging<br>- Repeat DES for fracture, collapse or edge dissection<br>- High-pressure non-compliant balloon for under-expansion or malapposition<br>(TSRA: early, back to the catheter lab)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

## 7. FFR: principle

<details class="fc"><summary>What is FFR and how is it measured?</summary><div class="fca">- Ratio of pressure distal to the lesion (0.014 inch pressure wire) to aortic pressure (catheter tip), transduced simultaneously<br>- Measured at maximal hyperaemia<br>(TSRA calls it the &#x27;pressure differential&#x27; between aorta and coronary; the Pd/Pa label appears in the notes only for the resting index)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which drugs induce hyperaemia for FFR, and what if adenosine is contraindicated?</summary><div class="fca">- Adenosine or regadenoson (TSRA)<br>- Papaverine if adenosine is contraindicated: risk of polymorphic VT (ESC, in the invasive functional testing protocol)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is global FFR and its significance?</summary><div class="fca">- Sum of FFR in the three vessels; normal 3 (1+1+1)<br>- Borderline FFR with global FFR &lt;2.72 had higher risk<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What does an FFR pullback add?</summary><div class="fca">- Pattern (focal versus diffuse) helps select patients likely to benefit from PCI<br>- iFR pullback or QFR mapping predicts post-PCI pressure and changes the plan in about 30%<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Write the full FFR equation and explain the usual simplification.</summary><div class="fca">FFR = (Pd - Pv) / (Pa - Pv) at maximal hyperaemia (Pv = central venous or right atrial pressure).<br>- Pv is negligibly low compared with arterial pressure, so FFR = Pd/Pa<br>- Toth 2016: adding right atrial pressure not indicated in any circumstances, even heart failure; Peper 2021: raised RA pressure has negligible clinical impact<br>(Older: Blows 2007 measured RA routinely; ignoring it reclassified 14% of 62 patients&#x27; lesions as insignificant)<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds); Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Give the adenosine doses and routes for FFR.</summary><div class="fca">- IV infusion 140 micrograms/kg/min (some use 180); central or peripheral vein: central fluctuates less and peaks faster, no accuracy difference<br>- IC bolus: 100 micrograms RCA, 200 micrograms left (&gt;95% of maximal hyperaemia; higher doses add AV block, not benefit)<br>- Use IV for ostial lesions, serial lesions, pullbacks and side-hole guides<br>(Older: Blows 2007 IC 15 to 20 micrograms RCA, 18 to 24 left)<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the contraindications to IV adenosine for FFR?</summary><div class="fca">Relative contraindications:<br>- Second- or third-degree AV block<br>- Sick sinus syndrome without a pacemaker<br>- Prolonged QT<br>- Severe hypotension; heart failure<br>- Obstructive pulmonary disease<br>(No absolute contraindication to FFR measurement itself)<small>Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the drawbacks of adenosine for the patient?</summary><div class="fca">- Often poorly tolerated despite its short half-life<br>- Adverse procedural symptoms 30.8% with FFR versus 3.1% with iFR (DEFINE-FLAIR)<br>- AV block, more with higher IC doses<br>- Short half-life gives only a brief window of maximal hyperaemia<br>(Individual symptoms such as dyspnoea or flushing not listed in the sources read)<small>Alisiddiq et al., Intra-coronary physiology in contemporary PCI, Front Cardiovasc Med 2023;10:1255643, doi 10.3389/fcvm.2023.1255643 (web, read in part: first two thirds); Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full); Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why give intracoronary nitrate before an FFR measurement?</summary><div class="fca">- Full epicardial vasodilatation and no spasm, so epicardial resistance is minimal and the gradient reflects the stenosis alone<br>- Toth 2016 table: 200 micrograms IC (one protocol: nitroglycerin 100 micrograms, repeated if the case runs past 30 min)<br>- Also mandatory: unfractionated heparin &gt;=50 U/kg<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds); Casadonte et al., Int J Cardiol 2018, doi 10.1016/j.ijcard.2018.08.051 (web, read in full: thesis chapter copy)</small></div></details>

## 8. FFR: thresholds and recommendations

<details class="fc"><summary>What FFR value defines a haemodynamically significant stenosis, and what class?</summary><div class="fca">FFR &lt;=0.80.<br>- ESC 2024: selective assessment of intermediate stenoses by FFR/iFR to guide revascularisation, I A<br>- Evidence: FAME 1, FAME 2, R3F, RIPCORD<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS Rec Table 12; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Should every vessel be interrogated with a pressure wire?</summary><div class="fca">No: routine wire-based assessment of all vessels is Class III A (RIPCORD2, FUTURE showed no outcome benefit). <details class="fc"><summary>What is the ESC 2024 recommendation on pressure-wire availability at angiography?</summary><div class="fca">Coronary pressure assessment available and used to assess intermediate non-LM stenoses before revascularisation: I A.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024) Rec Table 11</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the role of physiology at the end of PCI?</summary><div class="fca">- A quarter have residual ischaemia (FFR &lt;0.80 or iFR &lt;=0.89) after angiographically successful PCI; about 80% from focal lesions missed on angiography<br>- After further intervention it stays &lt;=0.80 in 18%<br>- FFR/iFR/QFR at end of PCI: identify high-risk patients IIa B; find lesions for more PCI IIb B<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the FFR grey zone and how do you handle a value in it?</summary><div class="fca">0.75 to 0.80 (Pijls: 0.76 to 0.80). ## 9. iFR and other resting indices

<details class="fc"><summary>What is iFR and its threshold?</summary><div class="fca">- Instantaneous wave-free ratio: resting, no hyperaemic drug, measured automatically in the wave-free period of diastole<br>- Significant if &lt;=0.89 (ESC 2024 I A with FFR; TSRA)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are the two advantages of iFR over FFR (TSRA)?</summary><div class="fca">- Can assess serial lesions along a single coronary artery<br>- Less patient discomfort (no adenosine)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which trials validated iFR, and what did 5-year pooled follow-up show?</summary><div class="fca">DEFINE-FLAIR and iFR-SWEDEHEART.<br>5-year pooled:<br>- 2% absolute increase in all-cause death with iFR<br>- No more unplanned revascularisation or non-fatal MI<br>- Deferral 50% (iFR) v 45% (FFR); iFR-based deferral safe to 5 years<br>(TSRA ch. 52, older: outcomes &#x27;equivalent&#x27; to FFR)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Which other resting indices are accepted, and at what class?</summary><div class="fca">Resting Pd/Pa, dPR, RFR or angiography-derived vessel FFR as alternatives: IIb C.<br>(Their thresholds are not given in the notes.)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024) Rec Table 12</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Give the thresholds of the resting (non-hyperaemic) pressure ratios.</summary><div class="fca">- iFR &lt;=0.89 (chosen to match FFR 0.80)<br>- RFR &lt;=0.89 (VALIDATE-RFR)<br>- DFR &lt;=0.89 (numerically equivalent to iFR)<br>- Resting Pd/Pa &lt;=0.91 (RESOLVE)<br>- dPR: mean Pd/Pa over all of diastole, correlates with iFR &gt;0.99; its cut-off is not stated in the sources read<small>Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full); Alisiddiq et al., Intra-coronary physiology in contemporary PCI, Front Cardiovasc Med 2023;10:1255643, doi 10.3389/fcvm.2023.1255643 (web, read in part: first two thirds)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What are RFR and resting Pd/Pa, and how well are they validated?</summary><div class="fca">- RFR: lowest Pd/Pa across the whole cardiac cycle (Abbott; DFR is Boston Scientific&#x27;s diastolic index); VALIDATE-RFR (651 iFR tracings, retrospective): R2 0.985 with iFR, equivalent; no RCT data<br>- Resting Pd/Pa: about 80% accuracy against FFR, r 0.97 with iFR; iFR more sensitive to stenosis severity<br>- Pd/Pa &lt;=0.91: 2-year MACE 8.7% v 1.5%<br>- iFR and FFR discordant in about 20% of borderline lesions, without outcome difference<small>Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full); Alisiddiq et al., Intra-coronary physiology in contemporary PCI, Front Cardiovasc Med 2023;10:1255643, doi 10.3389/fcvm.2023.1255643 (web, read in part: first two thirds)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Where are resting indices least reliable?</summary><div class="fca">- Drift: an absolute error (mmHg) matters more at small resting gradients<br>- Left main and proximal LAD: most iFR-FFR discordance (81% agreement in 91 LM lesions; no long-term LM outcome data)<br>- ACS non-culprits: higher resting flow may overstate severity; iFR rises 0.01 to 0.02 by follow-up<br>- Aortic stenosis: lower iFR cut-off (0.82 to 0.83) suggested<br>- RCA (lower sensitivity), heart rate, myocardial bridging<small>Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full); Pijls and De Bruyne, Fractional flow reserve, coronary pressure wires, and drift, Circ J 2016;80:1704-6, doi 10.1253/circj.CJ-16-0623 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

## 10. Angiography- and CT-derived FFR

<details class="fc"><summary>What is QFR, its threshold and class?</summary><div class="fca">- Quantitative flow ratio: angiography-derived, wireless<br>- Significant if &lt;=0.8<br>- ESC 2024: I B to guide revascularisation of intermediate stenoses<br>- FAVOR III China: better outcomes than angiography guidance (fewer MI and ischaemia-driven revascularisation)<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

## 11. Pressure-wire pitfalls

<details class="fc"><summary>Why is FFR unreliable in severe aortic stenosis?</summary><div class="fca">- Coronary flow and FFR are altered by valve disease; reduced coronary flow reserve from hypertrophy and raised wall stress<br>- ESC 2024 CCS: FFR/iFR unreliable, especially in AS<br>- ESC/EACTS 2025: interpret with caution; functional assessment not well established<small>Cardiac 5; Cardiac 9; ESC 2024 CCS; 2025 ESC/EACTS VHD</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What is the problem with serial lesions in one vessel?</summary><div class="fca">iFR can assess serial lesions along a single coronary artery, listed by TSRA as an advantage over FFR. The pullback pattern (focal versus diffuse) helps select patients likely to benefit from PCI (ESC 2024).<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52; ESC 2024 CCS</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Abnormal FFR with normal CFR: what does it mean?</summary><div class="fca">- Good outcome to 5 years untreated<br>- FFR and CFR concordant normal: excellent prognosis<br>- HSR (gradient divided by flow) is a strong diagnostic and prognostic index; CFR, HSR or CFC complementary IIa B<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Can a physiology number alone decide revascularisation?</summary><div class="fca">No: thresholds must be weighed with symptoms and non-invasive results (ESC 2024). Lower values carry proportionally more risk, and proximal lesions more risk for a given FFR.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Talk me through setting up a pressure-wire measurement.</summary><div class="fca">- Heparin, IC nitrate; zero both transducers to atmosphere, flush all lines<br>- Sensor 1 to 2 mm beyond the guide tip: pressures identical, else equalise (ostial disease: disengage, equalise in the aorta); wait 20 to 30 s for air to clear<br>- Advance sensor &gt;=2 to 3 cm beyond the stenosis (or to the planned anastomosis site); document position<br>- Record &gt;=30 s after contrast, &gt;=10 baseline beats, then hyperaemia; read FFR at the Pd/Pa nadir<br>- Keep the raw tracings; do not trust the console number alone<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Pijls and De Bruyne, Fractional flow reserve, coronary pressure wires, and drift, Circ J 2016;80:1704-6, doi 10.1253/circj.CJ-16-0623 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How do you check for drift and what is acceptable?</summary><div class="fca">- After every measurement pull the sensor back to 1 to 2 mm beyond the guide tip: pressures should match<br>- Drift &gt;3 mmHg: re-equalise and repeat (Pijls); Pd/Pa 0.97 to 1.03 commonly accepted<br>- FDA allows &lt;5 mmHg/10 min (Pijls: too liberal)<br>- Apparent drift can come from the guide: flush 5 to 10 mL saline<br>- Matters more for resting indices (hyperaemia increases the gradient 2.5 to 3 times)<small>Pijls and De Bruyne, Fractional flow reserve, coronary pressure wires, and drift, Circ J 2016;80:1704-6, doi 10.1253/circj.CJ-16-0623 (web, read in full); Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Casadonte et al., Int J Cardiol 2018, doi 10.1016/j.ijcard.2018.08.051 (web, read in full: thesis chapter copy)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How can the guide catheter falsify an FFR?</summary><div class="fca">- Wedging or ventricularisation of Pa during hyperaemia: catheter impedes ostial flow, falsely high FFR; watch the aortic waveform, disengage slightly as hyperaemia starts and after an IC bolus<br>- Damping from residual contrast (more with 5 Fr): flush with saline; dicrotic notch should be visible<br>- Side holes: IC drug escapes and calibration suffers; avoid, or use IV adenosine and disengage<br>- Diagnostic catheters discouraged: no reliable aortic waveform<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full); Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why remove the introducer needle from the Y-connector?</summary><div class="fca">- A leaking introducer loses adenosine and lowers Pa<br>- Remove it for equalisation, measurement and the drift check<br>- In for one reading and out for another explains a 5 to 10 mmHg difference<br>- Flush IC adenosine with adenosine, not saline or contrast (draw 2 to 3 mL extra for the dead space)<small>Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Pijls and De Bruyne, Fractional flow reserve, coronary pressure wires, and drift, Circ J 2016;80:1704-6, doi 10.1253/circj.CJ-16-0623 (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How do serial lesions distort FFR, and how do you deal with them?</summary><div class="fca">- A distal lesion reduces flow and the gradient across the proximal one: proximal FFR overestimated<br>- A proximal lesion lowers the ratio for the distal one<br>- True individual values need coronary wedge pressure<br>- In practice: slow pullback under steady IV adenosine (15 to 20 s), marking landmarks; abrupt step = focal, gradual rise = diffuse<br>- Side branches can cause steal<small>Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full); Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How do you assess a left main stenosis with downstream disease?</summary><div class="fca">- Interrogate the left main only if the sensor can sit in an unobstructed downstream vessel<br>- FFR &gt;0.80 in an intermediate left main safely defers CABG<br>- Stenoses proximal or distal to a side branch alter its FFR<br>- Resting indices: greatest iFR-FFR discordance in left main and proximal LAD<small>Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full); Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds); Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Can you use FFR in acute MI: culprit and non-culprit?</summary><div class="fca">- Culprit: no. <details class="fc"><summary>Name the causes of a falsely reassuring (high) FFR.</summary><div class="fca">- Submaximal hyperaemia (smaller gradient)<br>- Guide wedging, ventricularisation or damping<br>- Downstream serial lesion (overestimates the proximal FFR)<br>- Acute MI culprit (high microvascular resistance)<br>- Aortic stenosis (submaximal hyperaemic response); LVH, diabetes, microvascular disease (cut-off may be too low)<br>(Falsely low: accordion effect in tortuous vessels)<small>Blows and Redwood, The pressure wire in practice, Heart 2007;93:419-22 (epub 2006), doi 10.1136/hrt.2005.066837, PMID 16670101 (web, read in full); Toth et al., Standardization of fractional flow reserve measurements, JACC 2016;68:742-53, doi 10.1016/j.jacc.2016.05.067 (web, read in full: author repository copy); Peper et al., Fractional flow reserve: patient selection and perspectives, Vasc Health Risk Manag 2021;17:817-31, doi 10.2147/VHRM.S286916 (web, read in part: first two thirds); Michail et al., Non-hyperaemic pressure ratios to guide PCI, Open Heart 2020;7:e001308 (DOI not printed on page) (web, read in full)</small></div></details>

## 12. Microvascular disease (ANOCA/INOCA)

<details class="fc"><summary>What invasive thresholds define coronary microvascular dysfunction?</summary><div class="fca">- CFR (Doppler or thermodilution) &lt;2.5 (CMD threshold 2.0 to 2.5; thermodilution &lt;2.0 insensitive)<br>- IMR &gt;=25 U<br>- HMR (Doppler) &gt;2.5 mmHg/cm/s<br>- MRR &lt;2.7<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How is IMR calculated?</summary><div class="fca">Distal pressure at hyperaemia x hyperaemic mean transit time. Abnormal if &gt;=25 U.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>When is invasive coronary functional testing indicated (ESC 2024)?</summary><div class="fca">Persistent symptoms despite treatment, suspected ANOCA/INOCA (normal or non-obstructive arteries, or intermediate stenoses with normal FFR/iFR), and poor QoL: I B.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024) Rec Table 25</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>How is epicardial spasm distinguished from microvascular spasm on ACh testing?</summary><div class="fca">- Epicardial: symptoms, ischaemic ECG changes and &gt;=90% lumen reduction<br>- Microvascular: symptoms and ECG changes with &lt;90% reduction<br>- Pitfall: ACh bradycardia (RCA or dominant circumflex); atropine reverses it<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

## 13. Trials

<details class="fc"><summary>What did COURAGE show?</summary><div class="fca">2007: PCI added to optimal medical therapy in stable CAD did not reduce death or non-fatal MI over median 4.6 years. Spurred DES and FFR/iFR-guided PCI.<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What do the notes say about FAME 1 and FAME 2?</summary><div class="fca">- Cited as the evidence base for FFR &lt;=0.80 (with R3F, RIPCORD); FAME also showed poor visual-FFR correlation<br>- TSRA: physiology-guided PCI improved outcomes mainly by reducing urgent revascularisation (reading list: Xaplanteris NEJM 2018, 5-year FFR-guided PCI)<br>- Designs and figures not given in the notes<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What was the design of FAME 3?</summary><div class="fca">1,500 patients with three-vessel CAD, no left main; FFR-guided PCI with durable-polymer zotarolimus DES versus CABG.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What were the 1- and 3-year results of FAME 3?</summary><div class="fca">- 1 year MACCE: 10.6% PCI v 6.9% CABG; non-inferiority not met<br>- 3 years MACCE: 18.6% v 12.5% (HR 1.5)<br>- Death, MI or stroke at 3 years: 12.0% v 9.2% (P = .07)<br>- MI 7.0% v 4.2%; repeat revascularisation 11.1% v 5.9%<br>- SYNTAX &lt;=22: PCI as good as CABG<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did FAME 3 show at 5 years?</summary><div class="fca">- Death, stroke or MI 16% PCI v 14% CABG (HR 1.16; 0.89 to 1.52): no significant difference<br>- MI higher with PCI (HR 1.57)<br>- Repeat revascularisation 16% v 8%<br>- Left main excluded; diabetes and higher complexity still favour CABG<small>Cardiac archive (FAME 3 five-year, Lancet 2025, confirmed 17 Nov 2026); Cardiac 5 (FRCS Atlas notes)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did NOTION-3 show?</summary><div class="fca">455 patients with severe symptomatic AS and stable CAD (&gt;=1 stenosis &gt;=90% or FFR &lt;=0.80 in a &gt;=2.5 mm segment): routine PCI reduced death, MI or urgent revascularisation at median 2 years, driven by &gt;=90% lesions; more bleeding.<small>Cardiac 20; Cardiac 10; 2025 ESC/EACTS VHD</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did the TCW trial compare?</summary><div class="fca">FFR-guided PCI plus TAVI versus SAVR plus CABG: fewer deaths and major bleeds at 12 months, but stopped early, small, low prevalence of complex CAD.<small>Cardiac 20; Cardiac 4; 2025 ESC/EACTS VHD</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What was the design of FAME (FAME 1)?</summary><div class="fca">- 1,005 patients with multivessel disease: &gt;50% stenoses in at least two of three major vessels<br>- Excluded left main, prior CABG, recent STEMI, shock<br>- FFR-guided PCI (stent only if FFR &lt;=0.80; n 509) versus angiography-guided PCI (stent every &gt;50% lesion; n 496), DES<br>- Primary endpoint: death, MI or repeat revascularisation at 1 year<small>FAME: Tonino et al., NEJM 2009;360:213-24, PMID 19144937 (paper not read; web, ACC clinical trial summary page only)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What were the results of FAME (FAME 1)?</summary><div class="fca">- 1-year MACE 13.2% FFR v 18.3% angiography (P 0.02); death, MI and repeat revascularisation each lower, none significant alone<br>- Fewer stents (1.9 v 2.7), less contrast (272 v 302 mL), lower cost, same procedure time<br>- About a third of angiographically indicated lesions had FFR &gt;0.80<br>- 5-year MACE 28% v 31% (RR 0.91, NS)<br>- Post-PCI FFR &gt;=0.92: lowest vessel-related event rate<small>FAME: Tonino et al., NEJM 2009;360:213-24, PMID 19144937 (paper not read; web, ACC clinical trial summary page only)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What was the design of FAME 2, and why did it stop early?</summary><div class="fca">- Stable CAD suitable for PCI, 1 to 3 vessels; 28 centres<br>- At least one stenosis with FFR &lt;=0.80: randomised to FFR-guided PCI (DES) plus best medical therapy (n 447) or best medical therapy alone (n 441)<br>- FFR &gt;0.80 in all vessels: medical therapy registry<br>- Primary: death, MI or urgent revascularisation at 2 years<br>- Stopped January 2012 on DSMB advice: highly significant difference in the primary endpoint<small>FAME 2: De Bruyne et al., NEJM 2012;367:991-1001, doi 10.1056/NEJMoa1205361 (web, read in full: repository PDF)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What were the main results of FAME 2?</summary><div class="fca">- Primary endpoint 4.3% PCI v 12.7% medical (HR 0.32, 0.19 to 0.53)<br>- Driven by urgent revascularisation 1.6% v 11.1% (HR 0.13); death and MI no different<br>- Urgent revascularisation triggered by MI or ischaemic ECG: 0.9% v 5.2%<br>- Registry (FFR &gt;0.80): primary endpoint 3.0%: non-significant lesions do well on medical therapy<small>FAME 2: De Bruyne et al., NEJM 2012;367:991-1001, doi 10.1056/NEJMoa1205361 (web, read in full: repository PDF)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did FAME 2 show at 5 years?</summary><div class="fca">- Primary endpoint 13.9% PCI v 27.0% medical (HR 0.46, 0.34 to 0.63)<br>- Urgent revascularisation 6.3% v 21.1% (HR 0.27): the driver<br>- Death 5.1% v 5.2%; MI 8.1% v 12.0% (HR 0.66, not significant after correction)<br>- 51% of the medical arm had PCI by 5 years<br>- Registry 15.7%: similar to the PCI arm<small>FAME 2 five-year: Xaplanteris et al., NEJM 2018;379:250-9, doi 10.1056/NEJMoa1803538 (web, read in full: repository PDF)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What was the design of FAVOR IV-QVAS?</summary><div class="fca">- 793 patients for elective aortic and/or mitral valve surgery with &gt;=1 major coronary stenosis &gt;=50% (vessel &gt;=1.5 mm); 12 Chinese centres, 2019 to 2024; triple-blind, 1:1<br>- Angiography-derived FFR (AngioFFR, QFR-type; registered as QFR-guided): graft only vessels &lt;=0.80<br>- versus anatomical: graft every &gt;=50% stenosis<br>- Primary: 30-day death, MI, stroke, unplanned revascularisation or new dialysis<small>FAVOR IV-QVAS: Zhu et al., Lancet 2026;407:1161-70, PMID 41864747, NCT03977129 (paper and abstract not read; web, CareNet summary of the Lancet abstract and Medicom AHA 2025 report only)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What did FAVOR IV-QVAS show?</summary><div class="fca">- Concomitant CABG performed in 56% (physiology) v 98% (anatomy)<br>- 30-day primary endpoint 7.8% v 13.4% (RR 0.58, 0.38 to 0.89; P 0.01), driven by MI 1.5% v 5.6%<br>- 30-day death 2.8% v 4.3%<br>- Median 27 months composite 20.7% v 26.8% (HR 0.74, 0.55 to 0.98)<br>- Challenges the anatomical approach current guidelines recommend at valve surgery<small>FAVOR IV-QVAS: Zhu et al., Lancet 2026;407:1161-70, PMID 41864747, NCT03977129 (paper and abstract not read; web, CareNet summary of the Lancet abstract and Medicom AHA 2025 report only)</small></div></details>

## 14. Intracoronary imaging: IVUS and OCT

<details class="fc"><summary>When does ESC 2024 recommend IVUS or OCT guidance for PCI?</summary><div class="fca">Anatomically complex lesions, especially left main stem, true bifurcations and long lesions: I A.<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What does IVUS check after stent deployment (TSRA)?</summary><div class="fca">- Areas of ongoing stenosis or kinking in the stented segment<br>- Adequate stent expansion<br>- Coronary injury proximal or distal to the stent (edge injury)<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 52</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Name the intracoronary imaging trials and results.</summary><div class="fca">- RENOVATE-COMPLEX-PCI (74% IVUS): cardiac death, TV-MI or TVR at 2 years 7.7% v 12.3% (HR 0.64)<br>- OCTOBER (OCT, true bifurcation): 10.1% v 14.1% (HR 0.70)<br>- ILUMIEN IV (OCT): target-vessel failure 7.4% v 8.2% (NS); stent thrombosis 0.5% v 1.4%<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

## 15. Physiology and the surgeon

<details class="fc"><summary>How should a surgeon read a coronary angiogram when planning CABG?</summary><div class="fca">- Read anatomy, physiology and graftability separately<br>- Match targets, territories and conduits<br>- Consider competitive flow and prior radial artery access<small>Cardiac 5 (FRCS Atlas teaching notes; not a guideline)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why does competitive flow matter for graft choice?</summary><div class="fca">- Technical factors and competitive flow drive early graft failure (progression and degeneration drive late failure)<br>- Radial patency (83 to 95% at 5 years) is affected by competitive flow<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; TSRA ch. 50</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>What target stenosis suits a radial artery graft?</summary><div class="fca">A tightly stenosed non-LAD target: over 70%, ideally 90%. It is the best-supported second arterial graft (practice point, not marked as confirmed).<small>Cardiac archive (practice points)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>At redo CABG, what do you do with a diseased old vein graft when adding an arterial graft?</summary><div class="fca">Leave the old vein graft intact if its stenosis is &gt;50% to avoid competitive flow; place the new distal anastomosis on the native vessel.<small>Cardiac 5; TSRA ch. 50</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why does physiology matter for completeness of revascularisation?</summary><div class="fca">- Functional completeness is what counts: anatomically incomplete but functionally complete PCI did better than anatomically complete PCI (SYNTAX)<br>- ESC 2024 revascularisation recommendations refer to &#x27;functionally significant&#x27; disease<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS (Vrints, EHJ 2024)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>Why does CABG prevent more spontaneous infarcts than PCI?</summary><div class="fca">A graft to the mid-vessel protects myocardium beyond any future proximal plaque rupture (&#x27;surgical collateralisation&#x27;); PCI treats the focal lesion only. (FRCS Atlas draft; not checked against a primary source.)<small>Cardiac 5 (FRCS Atlas additions; unchecked)</small></div></details>

<details class="fc"><summary>A patient needs aortic valve surgery and has a 60% LAD stenosis: can you use FFR to decide whether to graft?</summary><div class="fca">- FFR/iFR are unreliable in valve disease, especially AS<br>- ESC 2024: CABG recommended with a primary aortic, mitral or tricuspid indication and significant coronary stenosis<br>- PCI before TAVI or transcatheter mitral intervention considered if proximal stenosis &gt;70%<small>Cardiac 5; ESC 2024 CCS; 2025 ESC/EACTS VHD</small></div></details>

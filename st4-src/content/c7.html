How well does it work? (as taught, slides 18 November 2026)

Complete lesion sets restore sinus rhythm best; an appendage clip alone rarely does. The figures and citations below are copied from the slide and were not re-checked by me, apart from the CTSN trial, whose design I know from memory (checked 25 November 2026 against the ACC trial summary of Gillinov AM et al, N Engl J Med 2015, and the STS 2023 summary in this tab: 260 patients with persistent or long-standing persistent AF at mitral surgery, randomised to ablation (then to pulmonary vein isolation or biatrial maze) or none, all with appendage closure; freedom from AF at 12 months 63% against 29%; vein isolation 61% against biatrial maze 66%, P = 0.60; pacemakers 21.5 against 8.1 per 100 patient-years. The other slide figures are still unchecked).

Reading it: within the CTSN trial the lesion set did not matter but ablating did; across the cohort data the posterior wall (box) is what separates good from poor results. A clip treats stroke risk, not rhythm.

Left atrial appendage techniques (as taught)

Close the appendage at its base and confirm on TOE.

Technique Notes Reliability
Amputation and double-layer oversew Excise at the base and close; standard in Cox-Maze; watch the circumflex and bleeding High when closed at the base
Stapled excision Fast, off-pump; risk of tearing friable tissue and of a residual stump Good; check for stump
Epicardial clip (for example AtriClip) Placed at the base, often on the beating heart; electrically isolates the appendage High; low stump and leak rates
Internal suture ligation Purse-string or running suture from inside through the atriotomy Poor: frequent incomplete closure or recanalisation

Slide sources: ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS 2023 guideline; EHRA/HRS/APHRS/LAHRS 2024 consensus; ICVTS 2025 on internal suture closure. This agrees with LAAOS III, which did not allow purse-string closure alone (from memory; checked in part 25 November 2026: the LAAOS III design paper, Whitlock R et al, Ann Cardiothorac Surg 2014;3:45–54, read through a page reader, permits amputation and closure (preferred) or stapler closure, with devices only if the steering committee approves, and the STS 2023 summary in this tab adds double-layer internal suture and clip as used in the trial; neither states in words that purse-string closure alone was excluded).

Surgical treatment of atrial fibrillation

3 parts

Every patient with AF having a first-time elective cardiac operation should have the appendage occluded and should be considered for ablation; the randomised evidence is strongest for the appendage.

Trials

Trial Design Result
LAAOS III, NEJM 2021 4,811 patients with AF and CHA₂DS₂-VASc 2 or more having cardiac surgery, 105 centres; appendage occlusion or not, both continuing anticoagulation Ischaemic stroke or systemic embolism 4.8% against 7.0% (HR 0.67, 0.53–0.85) over a mean of 3.8 years; benefit mainly after 30 days; no excess bleeding, heart failure or death

Practice points

  • LAAOS III tested occlusion in addition to anticoagulation, not instead of it. Do not stop anticoagulation because the appendage is closed.

  • Technique in the trial: amputation and closure in over half, stapler or double-layer internal suture in the rest. Purse-string or simple ligation alone was not allowed, because incomplete closure is common.

  • Confirm closure on TOE: no flow and a stump under 1 cm.

  • Lesion sets: pulmonary vein isolation for paroxysmal AF only; a left atrial or biatrial Cox-Maze set for persistent AF, with every line transmural and anchored to an annulus or another line.

  • Bipolar radiofrequency clamps for the vein and box lesions; cryoablation for lines ending on the mitral or tricuspid annulus.

  • Biatrial lesions give the best rhythm results and the highest pacemaker rate.

  • After surgery: anticoagulate, antiarrhythmic for 2–3 months, and document rhythm on a monitor at 6–12 months.

How to read it

  1. It tests occlusion added to anticoagulation, not instead of it. That is why the guideline wording is "as an adjunct to oral anticoagulation".

  2. It applies to patients already having cardiac surgery. It says nothing about percutaneous devices or about stand-alone closure.

  3. The benefit appears after the first month, so it is a long-term gain for a few minutes of clamp time.

  4. The old worry that removing the appendage causes heart failure (loss of natriuretic peptide and reservoir function) was not borne out.

  5. Guideline effect: the 2024 ESC/EACTS guideline makes surgical closure Class I, level B, in patients with AF having cardiac surgery (see the section above).

How to use these in a viva

  1. Evidence today: appendage closure is proven only in patients with known AF (LAAOS III). Closing it in patients without AF is not yet supported.

  2. LeAAPS will answer the stroke question in patients without AF; LAACS-2 is a second trial of the same idea (from memory) (corrected 25 November 2026: LAACS-2 has reported, at ESC Congress 2026 on 31 August 2026: 1,500 patients having first-time open-heart surgery at four sites in Denmark, Spain and Sweden, only 4.1% with known AF; stroke or TIA 4.28% with closure against 5.04% at a median 4.0 years, HR 0.85 (0.53 to 1.37), p = 0.52, not significant; a benefit only in patients with above-median CHA2DS2-VASc, a subgroup finding. Read from a News-Medical report of the ESC press release; the paper was not seen).

  3. BoxX-NoAF asks a different question, rhythm not stroke, and adds an ablation to patients who have never had AF; the harms to watch are added time, bleeding and pacemaker need (my comment).

  4. Both are industry-sponsored device trials: say so.
